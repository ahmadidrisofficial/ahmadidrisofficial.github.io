Why Pathways exists

Sign-ups are easy to celebrate and easy to fake. A form filled in, a seat booked, a course started. None of it means anything actually changed for the person who did it. What changes something is finishing: staying long enough for the thing to work.

I built education technology in Nigeria, and I now teach and lead in UK higher education. I keep meeting the same gap in both places, so I built a short test I ask before trying to fix it. Each week, one question from that test, and what it looks like tried on something real.

Issue 1: Why I care more about finishers than sign-ups

Sent Sunday 4 October 2026. A new issue lands every Sunday morning; past issues stay here.

Hi friend,

In a few hours I stand at a start line for the first time as a registered club athlete. I turned 35 last week, and running my first race for West End Runners is the present I gave myself: permission to try something I quietly wanted since I was fourteen, and never quite let go of.

I am not telling you this because Pathways is a running newsletter. I am telling you because of what happened between turning 35 and this morning. I trained quietly from January. Nobody watched. Most people who start something like this do not finish it, and I very nearly did not either.

That is what this letter is about from now on. Not a diary of my week, not a list of events. One idea I keep running into wherever I look: education widens opportunity only when people finish, not when they sign up.

I saw it first in Nigeria, building coding programmes for schools in places genuinely short of options. Sign-ups were never the problem. Getting a child to still be there in week six was. I see the same thing now in the UK, teaching adults who come back to education after ten or twenty years away. They arrive. Many of them do not finish. The gap between those two facts is where I actually spend my working life, and it is what this letter is going to be about from here.

So this month I want to show you the five questions I ask before I try to fix anything like that. I call it The Finisher Test. Question one, this week: who exactly isn't finishing?

It is not one question, really. It is four, and most problems fall apart at the second one.

What exactly is the problem, in words you could actually watch happen? Not "engagement is low," but something like "second year students stop handing in drafts after week four."

Who has it, specifically? Not "students." Which students? This is where most problems fall apart, because the honest answer is usually "we are not sure."

What do we actually know, and what are we guessing? Write the guesses down separately. They are the dangerous part.

What is the smallest test that would tell us if we are right? Small enough to try next week, not next year.

I learned this way of thinking from Bill Aulet's Disciplined Entrepreneurship, a method I picked up at MIT that was built for founders choosing which customer to start with. It turns out it works just as well pointed at a classroom, or at a course nobody is finishing.

Try this in the week ahead: pick one thing you or someone near you keeps starting and not finishing. Work, home, wherever it shows up. Answer question two in one sentence: who, specifically, has this problem? Not "everyone." Not "me, sometimes." One honest sentence.

On the cohort, briefly: last month Steamledge and DataCamp opened 150 free scholarship places on a data and AI course. This month I am going to do to my own work exactly what I am asking you to do to yours, and watch, honestly, whether people actually finish it. I do not have real totals to share with you yet. Once our partner and I have agreed what I can say publicly, I will give you the actual numbers, good or bad, not a tidied-up version of them. That is the whole point of this exercise, so I am not going to skip the part where it might not look good.

One question back before I go. What did you start and not finish? What got in the way? Hit reply and tell me, I read every reply. And if this letter is no longer for you, just reply with the word unsubscribe and I will take you off the list, no hard feelings.

Right, I should go and find my running shoes.

Ahmad
